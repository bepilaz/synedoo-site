Analysis

So — who performed well,
and who didn't?

The 2023–24 Bundesliga produced one of the most extraordinary seasons in European football history. Yet the physical data raises the same uncomfortable question it always does: the numbers alone cannot tell you who was good and who was not.

Case 01 · The Unbeaten Champions

Bayer Leverkusen went the entire season undefeated — 28 wins and 6 draws. Their total distance of ~11,210 m per player per match sat below the league average, and their HSR output of ~498 m was mid-table.

They won the title by 6 points, unbeaten. The most dominant season in Bundesliga history — achieved with below-average physical volume.

Case 02 · High Output, Disappointing Return

Werder Bremen ranked among the top five in total distance per player (~11,520 m) and sustained their output across all 34 matchdays. Their sprint distance was also above the league average at ~262 m per player per match.

They finished 9th — four places below the European spots they targeted at the start of the season.

Case 03 · Explosive but Relegated

Darmstadt, relegated with the worst record in the division (17 pts, 30 goals scored), produced sprint distances competitive with established mid-table sides. Their players were willing — running above 25.2 km/h at a rate comparable to Freiburg and Mainz.

Sprint distance did not compensate for a lack of quality in possession, pressing structure, or individual decision-making.

Case 04 · RB Leipzig's Pressing Machine

RB Leipzig led the Bundesliga in sprint count and ranked highest in HSR distance — ~558 m per player per match above 19.8 km/h. Their pressing model under Marco Rose generated the highest-intensity physical profile in the division.

They finished 4th — above champions-elect Leverkusen in every physical metric, yet 25 points behind them in the table.

What the data cannot tell you

Physical metrics are necessary. But on their own, they answer the wrong question. A player running 12,000 m in a match where their team concedes four goals has produced very different value from a player running 10,500 m in a 1–0 win. The number looks better. The outcome is worse.

Data must be accurate first — collected cleanly, standardised across systems, free of noise. It must make sense second — situated within the tactical context of each match, each opponent, each phase of the season. And it must be contextualised with the people involved: the head of performance who knows why a player's sprint numbers dropped in March, the analyst who spotted that the HSR spike in December came from chasing games in the final ten minutes.
