Analysis

So — who performed well,
and who didn't?

It seems like a simple question. But if you look at the numbers alone, the honest answer is: it's impossible to say.

Case 01 · Effort ≠ Result

Burnley averaged 11,608 m per player per match — highest in the league. Sprint distance: 284 m per player. The hardest-working side by every measure.

They were relegated in 19th place.

Case 02 · Economy of Effort

Man City averaged 11,342 m — below the league average. HSR of 511 m per player ranked them mid-table for high-intensity output.

They won the title.

Case 03 · The Explosive Mid-Table

Tottenham led the league in sprint distance: 305 m per player per match. Their HSR of 584 m was also the highest in the division.

They finished 5th.

Case 04 · Low Intensity, High Return

Aston Villa recorded the lowest HSR in the top eight: 453 m per player. Total distance of 10,868 m ranked them near the bottom of the league.

They finished 4th. Champions League football.

What the data cannot tell you

Physical metrics are necessary. But on their own, they answer the wrong question. A player running 12,000 m in a match where their team concedes four goals has produced very different value from one running 10,500 m in a 1–0 win. The number looks better. The outcome is worse.

Data must be accurate first — collected cleanly, standardised across systems. It must make sense second — placed within the tactical and seasonal context of each match. And it must be contextualised with the people involved: the performance coach who knows why a player's numbers dropped in March, the analyst who flagged that the HSR spike came from chasing games in the final minutes.
